Conclusion

The results support the hypothesis that increasing slit separation produces a narrower fringe pattern, consistent with the inverse relationship given by y = λL / d.

At 0.5 mm, the measured pattern width (4.85 cm) closely matched the theoretical value (4.86 cm), with a percentage error of only 0.2%, indicating high accuracy and reliability in the method. At 1.0 mm, the measured width was 3.93 cm compared to a theoretical value of 3.16 cm, giving a larger error of 24.4%. This is likely due to difficulty in distinguishing closely spaced fringes, as well as measurement uncertainties such as parallax and slight misalignment.

For slit separations from 1.5 mm to 3.0 mm, no measurable fringe pattern was observed. This is consistent with theory, as increasing d reduces fringe spacing to below the resolution of the method.

Overall, the results demonstrate that fringe spacing decreases as slit separation increases, supporting the wave nature of light, although accuracy decreases as the fringes become harder to observe.

Analysis

The measured pattern widths were compared to theoretical values calculated using W = (λL / d) × (n − 1), where λ = 650 nm, L = 2.00 m, and n is the average fringe count. The percentage errors are shown in the table opposite. The 0.5 mm result was essentially exact (0.2% error), while the 1.0 mm result had a larger error of 24.4%.

One notable outlier was identified: in Trial 2 for d = 0.5 mm, only 14 fringes were counted compared to 18–22 in all other trials. This is likely because the outer fringes were too faint to distinguish clearly in that photograph. For d = 1.0 mm, the measured widths showed more variation across trials (3.3–4.8 cm), which increased the spread of results and contributed to the larger percentage error.

The 24.4% error for d = 1.0 mm is most likely due to difficulty identifying where the fringe pattern ended, since the closely spaced fringes for this separation blurred into the background diffraction glow more easily than for 0.5 mm. Other sources of error include parallax between the fringe pattern and the ruler, camera movement between shots, and the darkened room making precise ruler readings more difficult.

The fact that slit separations of 1.5 mm and above produced no measurable pattern is consistent with theory. At d = 1.5 mm, y = 0.87 mm, so fringes less than 1 mm apart are very difficult to distinguish and measure from a photograph taken at 2.00 m. This limitation could be reduced by moving the screen further away or using a higher-resolution camera.

Impact on Science

Thomas Young (1773–1829), who first performed the double-slit experiment in 1801.

When Thomas Young conducted the double-slit experiment in 1801, he achieved one of the most important results in the history of physics. At that time, Isaac Newton's particle theory of light had been the accepted view for over a century. Young's observation of interference patterns revealed that light acts as a wave. This finding challenged long-held scientific beliefs and showed how new evidence can change our understanding of the natural world.

This discovery established the basis for wave optics and has had lasting effects on modern technology. Lasers depend on the coherent, wave-like nature of light and are commonly used in medicine, manufacturing and communication systems. Optical fibres carry large amounts of data as light signals around the world with minimal loss, forming the backbone of the internet. Diffraction gratings, which rely on the same interference principles, are utilised in spectroscopy to analyse the makeup of substances in fields ranging from chemistry to astronomy.

The impact reaches even further into modern physics. The double-slit experiment showed that particles like electrons also create interference patterns, leading to the idea of wave-particle duality. This finding was crucial for the development of quantum mechanics, which supports technologies such as semiconductors, computers and smartphones.

Overall, this illustrates how a single scientific experiment can change both theoretical understanding and practical technology. It emphasises the importance of questioning existing ideas through experimentation and shows how fundamental discoveries can drive advancements that influence everyday life.

Reflection

Overall, the experiment worked well. We successfully produced and photographed interference fringe patterns for the two smallest slit separations (0.5 mm and 1.0 mm), and the results clearly showed that a wider slit separation produced more closely spaced fringes, which matched what the formula predicted. Completing six repeated trials for each separation also helped reduce the effect of random measurement errors on our final average.

One issue we noticed was that the fringe pattern was projected onto the wall above the ruler, rather than directly alongside it. This meant we had to estimate fringe positions relative to the ruler scale from an angle, introducing parallax error. If we repeated the experiment, we would tape the ruler directly at the height of the fringe pattern on the wall so that measurements could be read straight on.

Another problem was that the laser and ruler shifted slightly between trials: comparing the photographs, the fringe pattern appears at a different position on the ruler in each shot. This suggests the laser clamp or the ruler was not fully fixed. Using a clamp stand and taping the ruler firmly to the wall would prevent this in future.

The centre of the pattern was also overexposed in most photographs, making it difficult to distinguish individual bright fringes and to identify precisely where the pattern ended. This overexposure most likely caused us to overestimate the pattern width in several trials, particularly for d = 1.0 mm where the fringes are more closely spaced and the background glow is harder to separate from the true fringe edges. Trial 4 for d = 1.0 mm, which gave the closest result to the theoretical value, may have been measured more carefully or captured the pattern at a better exposure. In future we would reduce the camera exposure setting and measure only to the last clearly distinct fringe rather than the outermost visible glow.
