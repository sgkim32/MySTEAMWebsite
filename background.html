Project Background

Light diffracts through two slits and forms an interference pattern.

The double-slit experiment is a key part of wave optics. It was first conducted by the British polymath Thomas Young in 1801. At that time, the scientific community was split on the nature of light. Isaac Newton's "corpuscular" theory, which stated that light is made of particles, was the prevailing idea. Young's experiment provided the first clear evidence that light acts like a wave. The patterns observed could only be explained by the principles of interference and diffraction.

The Principle of Diffraction: In this experiment, a coherent light source, like a laser, is aimed at a barrier with two narrow, parallel slits. When the light passes through these slits, it undergoes diffraction, which is the spreading out of waves as they move through an opening or around an obstacle. Because the slits are very narrow, the light does not move in a straight line; instead, it spreads out into a wide arc. This allows the light waves from one slit to overlap with those from the other slit.

Wave Interference and Fringe Formation: When the two diffracted wave fronts meet, they interfere with each other. This interference follows the principle of superposition, creating a pattern of alternating bright and dark areas called fringes. A bright fringe appears through constructive interference, which happens at points where the waves arrive in sync, meaning that the crest of one wave aligns with the crest of another. In contrast, a dark fringe results from destructive interference, where the waves arrive out of sync. Here, the crest of one wave meets the trough of another, effectively cancelling the light.

The Mathematics of Interference: The shape of this pattern depends on the relationship between the wavelength of the light (λ), the distance from the slits to the screen (L) and the separation of the slits (d). The distance between individual bright fringes, known as fringe spacing (y), is given by the formula: y = (L × λ) / d. According to this equation, the slit separation is crucial in determining how close the fringes are. When the slits are farther apart, the path difference between the waves changes more quickly across the screen, causing the fringes to be closer together. Understanding this balance between wave interference and physical geometry is essential for studying the wave behaviour of light in a STEAM context.

What is Light?

A light wave showing wavelength (λ) and amplitude.

Light travels as an electromagnetic wave. Three properties describe it:

  • Wavelength (λ): distance between two peaks, determines colour
  • Frequency (f): wave cycles per second
  • Amplitude (A): height of the wave, determines brightness

The Electromagnetic Spectrum

Visible light is just a small part of the much larger electromagnetic spectrum. All electromagnetic waves travel at the speed of light but have different wavelengths:

The Visible Light Spectrum

The visible spectrum, from violet (380 nm) to red (700 nm).

Visible light splits into colours by wavelength. This is what a prism or rainbow shows:

  • Violet: ~380 nm (shortest visible wavelength)
  • Blue: ~450 nm
  • Green: ~530 nm
  • Yellow: ~580 nm
  • Orange: ~620 nm
  • Red: ~650 nm (our laser)

Diffraction

Waves spreading out after passing through a narrow slit.

Diffraction is when waves spread out after passing through a narrow gap.

  • Narrower slit: more spreading
  • Wider slit: less spreading

Each slit in our experiment acts as a new source of circular waves. These two sets of waves then overlap and interfere with each other.

Interference

Real double-slit interference fringes: bright and dark bands on the screen.

When waves from two slits overlap, they interfere:

  • Constructive interference: peaks meet peaks, producing a bright fringe
  • Destructive interference: peaks meet troughs, producing a dark fringe

This alternating pattern of bright and dark bands is direct evidence that light behaves as a wave.

The Key Formula

y = λL / d

As d increases, y decreases, so fringes get closer together.
  • y: fringe spacing between adjacent bright fringes
  • λ: wavelength (650 nm, red laser)
  • L: distance to screen (2.00 m)
  • d: slit separation (0.5 to 3.0 mm)

Because d is on the bottom of the fraction, making it bigger makes y smaller (an inverse relationship).

In our experiment, we measured the total pattern width W (the span across all visible fringes) rather than a single fringe spacing. W is related to y by W = y × (n − 1), where n is the number of visible bright fringes.
